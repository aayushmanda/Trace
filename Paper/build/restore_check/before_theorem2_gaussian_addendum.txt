A Transformer can represent a counting algorithm and still struggle to learn it from
ﬁnal answers. On counting modulo a small number, showing the running counts
makes the task much easier to learn. We explain this gap through the information
each training signal carries. Every letter hidden from a feature contracts its correlation with the ﬁnal count, while near initialization the Transformer’s output is dominated by features that read only a few letters. A displayed count exposes the next
update directly. In a two-block Transformer that holds exact solutions for both
formats, outcome gradient ﬂow from a zero readout stays near chance for a time
exponential in the number of letters. With a gate that reads the displayed count and
the current letter, joint gradient ﬂow from zero readouts and small nonzero gate
scalars along ﬁxed structured directions reaches positive margins on every transition in polynomial time. This holds for every modulus when match and non-match
probabilities differ; the proof supplies the routing and gate-readout direction. A
clean binary extension also learns soft suppression of designated zero-key, zerovalue distractors from diffuse attention in polynomial time. A full-preﬁx extension
learns the current-letter relative offset within a tied feature family and reaches a
prescribed step loss with ﬁxed group mobilities. Experiments test what happens
beyond these restrictions: step supervision helps on counting, other state-tracking
tasks, and LoRA-tuned Qwen2.5-0.5B on BIG-Bench Hard object tracking. It
does not help at every size, and one reversal remains unexplained.

1 Introduction
A model that can count need not learn to count from ﬁnal answers. In our experiments, the same
Transformer succeeds when shown running counts and stays near chance when trained on the ﬁnal
count alone. Yet we can write down exact parameters for either computation. If both solutions are
available, what does showing the steps give the learner?
Intermediate supervision often makes reasoning tasks easier to learn [Nye et al., 2021, Wei et al.,
2022, Lightman et al., 2023]. The usual intuition is that steps break a long computation into smaller
ones. We ask what this means for the gradient of a ﬁxed network. Counting a query letter modulo
K lets us examine that question closely: the answer-only and step-by-step tasks share the same
counting process, architecture, initialization and training budget (Figure 2b, Figure 1a).
The key is what a feature learns from the letters it reads. Averaging over every other letter contracts
its correlation with the ﬁnal count by a factor λ < 1 per unseen letter. Near initialization, the
output is dominated by terms that each read one letter; combining more letters costs powers of the
initialization scale (Figure 1b). Final answers thus supply very little credit to the features the network
can readily change. A displayed count and the current letter, however, expose the next update. The

Outcome: few-letter features cannot see the ﬁnal count

The network reads few letters at a time. Scale every attention score by ζ ∈ [0, 1] and expand
in ζ. At ζ = 0 attention is uniform and the MLPs are afﬁne, so the network is additive over
positions: each term reads at most one letter. Every interaction among letters passes through the
scores, and a term with k ≥ 1 scores reads at most 2k + 1 letters and costs ε2+4k in the Jacobian
(Proposition C.1). Applying Equation (3) term by term bounds the outcome credit. Call the credit
of the score-dependent part Jscore of the Jacobian the score-mediated credit: it contains every term
that reads two or more letters, and also score-dependent terms that read one.

Does outcome escape exponentially slowly? The plateau proof concerns continuous time and a
small ball. We ﬁrst check whether its escape mechanism is visible with ﬁnite updates. In a compact
model built to approximate the theorems’ assumptions, with full-batch gradient descent in ﬂoat64
and no ReLU sign change on any input along the trajectory, the outcome exit time grows at least as
fast as λ−n , both from the ball Bε of Theorem 3 and at a displacement of 0.1εkθ̃k, while the step
exit time stays constant (Figure 3a, Table 13), because the step loss can leave the ball by ﬁtting the
count-only prior; halving the update length changes the exit time by less than 1%. At initialization,
99.6% of the Jacobian’s energy is at degree 0 (Figure 1b), and the answer-dependent gradient decays
by a ﬁtted 0.48–0.51 per letter at λ = 0.5 over three seeds (Table 15).
Under AdamW, with a zero readout and paired seeds, at n = 16, K = 2 and 8k updates, step
training reaches 93.1% exact accuracy (8 of 10 seeds above 90%) and outcome training 49.8%
(none). Across 120 counting runs at n = 6–16 with the default readout, outcome training solves
none and step training 60 within 8k updates. Step training at ε = 0.02 is slow rather than stuck, as
its ε6 rule credit suggests: every step run of Table 17(a) is solved by 16k updates, and at ε = 0.1,
where ε6 is 1.6 × 104 times larger, every step seed solves within 8k. From initialization through 6k
outcome updates, at most 2.4 × 10−6 of the Jacobian’s energy lies above degree 1 (Appendix E.3).
Outcome failure is not universal at moderate λn : for counting modulo 3 at n = 8 (λn ≈ 0.036),
outcome training solves two of three seeds within 32k updates.

Intermediate supervision. Scratchpads and process supervision [Nye et al., 2021, Uesato et al.,
2022, Wei et al., 2022, Lightman et al., 2023] have been analysed through task decomposition [Wies
et al., 2023], locality [Prystawski et al., 2023, Abbe et al., 2024] and expressivity [Feng et al., 2023,
Merrill and Sabharwal, 2024, Malach, 2024]; outcome supervision need not be statistically harder
[Jia et al., 2025], and chain supervision can improve sample complexity [Hanneke et al., 2026, Wen
et al., 2025]; Wen et al. [2025] trace the gain to sparse dependence between tokens, which the
running count makes local. We study how a ﬁxed network distributes gradient credit.
Gradient signal for hard targets. Shalev-Shwartz et al. [2017] show exponentially weak targetdependent gradients over families of composed targets. We ask how one ﬁxed target supplies credit
to a concrete Transformer, by tracking which letters each network term reads. Staircase and leap
analyses [Abbe et al., 2021, 2022, 2023] characterize when SGD learns high-degree targets; for
counting at λ > 0 every degree is present, and its size, not its absence, limits credit. Abbe et al.
[2025] prove that noisy gradient descent on a two-layer network stays at chance on high-degree parities under Gaussian initialization, for the correlation loss; Theorem 3 concerns a Transformer under
cross-entropy, with time λ−n from the contraction of the target. Panigrahi et al. [2025] show that
intermediate teacher checkpoints supply low-degree signal for sparse parity; we analyse optimization rather than sample complexity. Li and Mossel [2025] use an Efron–Stein decomposition of the
target; our proofs need no decomposition: they bound the overlap of the target with each network
term directly, by conditioning on the letters the term reads, and we use ANOVA degree spectra only
as a measurement (Appendix E.3). Closest in spirit, Hahn and Roﬁn [2024] show that Transformers
are biased toward low-sensitivity functions, through a sharpness argument on the loss landscape; we
bound gradient credit through the letters each network term reads, and add a time bound. Spectral
analyses of simplicity bias [Yang and Salman, 2019] ﬁnd kernel eigenvalues on the Boolean cube
that decay with degree, consistent with the low-degree Jacobian we measure.

318

Chain of thought for parity and permutations. Kim and Suzuki [2025] prove that a one-layer
Transformer with position-only attention and a ﬁxed nonlinearity learns parity in one teacher-forced
step, and that gradient methods fail without intermediate supervision for a parity drawn from a class.
We ask why one format succeeds and the other fails for one ﬁxed target in a model holding both
solutions, with the computation learned. Wang et al. [2025] give a statistical-query lower bound and
a curriculum result for compositions of hidden permutations; our displayed states permit constantdepth executors. Counting modulo K is a cyclic-group automaton, for which Liu et al. [2023]
construct shallow shortcut Transformers; our outcome executor is such a shortcut, and the question
is whether training ﬁnds it.

Purpose and setup. These runs approximate the regime of (A1)–(A4): the architecture, task and
initialization conditions are enforced where they can be checked numerically, and gradient ﬂow
is approximated by reﬁned full-batch updates. They test exponential outcome escape and step rule
credit with polynomial horizon cost. The compact widths below test the gradient and exit mechanism
only; exact executors are certiﬁed by Proposition 1 in the wider architecture, not at these widths. We
use K = 2, two blocks of width 16, three heads of dimension 8, MLP width 16, |b̃m | ≥ 1, and
two letters with match probability 41 (λ = 12 ). Gradients are exact over all words through n = 14,
in full-batch ﬂoat64, with step objective Lstep . Exit-time runs start from a zero readout; credit
measurements retain a nonzero readout (Appendix C.5).
Each update moves the parameters by 10−3 εkθ̃k. The exit time τ (r) is the accumulated step size
when kθj − θ0 k = rεkθ̃k. We report it at r = 0.1 and at the radius r0 ε of the ball Bε in Theorem 3,
r = r0 /kθ̃k ≈ 1.8 × 10−3 (r0 = 81 and kθ̃k ≈ 70 for all three draws); τ (0.1) is therefore a
displacement time well outside Bε . Halving or quartering the update changes τ (0.1) by less than
1%. The exit from Bε is a much shorter distance, so it is integrated with updates of 10−6 εkθ̃k, about
1,800 updates to the boundary; updates of 10−5 change it by less than 0.5%. At every update before
either exit, no ReLU pre-activation changes sign from its value at θ0 , on any input or position, so
the ﬁxed activation pattern of the analysis holds along the trajectory.
