                                                         Abstract
1              A Transformer can represent a counting algorithm and still struggle to learn it
2              from ﬁnal answers. On counting modulo a small number, showing the running
3              counts makes the task much easier to learn. We explain this gap through the infor-
4              mation each training signal carries. Every letter hidden from a feature contracts its
5              correlation with the ﬁnal count, while near initialization the Transformer’s output
6              is dominated by features that read only a few letters. A displayed count exposes
7              the next update directly. In a two-block Transformer that holds exact solutions
8              for both formats, outcome gradient ﬂow from a zero readout stays near chance for
9              a time exponential in the number of letters. With a gate that reads the displayed
10             count and the current letter, simultaneous gradient ﬂow from small random magni-
11             tudes along structured directions learns the supervised transitions after polynomial
12             time. This holds for every modulus when match and non-match probabilities dif-
13             fer; the proof supplies the routing and gate-readout direction. Experiments test
14             what happens beyond these restrictions: step supervision helps on counting, other
15             state-tracking tasks, and LoRA-tuned Qwen2.5-0.5B on BIG-Bench Hard object
16             tracking. It does not help at every size, and one reversal remains unexplained.


17   1 Introduction
18   A model that can count need not learn to count from ﬁnal answers. In our experiments, the same
19   Transformer succeeds when shown running counts and stays near chance when trained on the ﬁnal
20   count alone. Yet we can write down exact parameters for either computation. If both solutions are
21   available, what does showing the steps give the learner?
22   Intermediate supervision often makes reasoning tasks easier to learn [Nye et al., 2021, Wei et al.,
23   2022, Lightman et al., 2023]. The usual intuition is that steps break a long computation into smaller
24   ones. We ask what this means for the gradient of a ﬁxed network. Counting a query letter modulo
25   K lets us examine that question closely: the answer-only and step-by-step tasks share the same
26   counting process, architecture, initialization and training budget (Figure 2b, Figure 1a).
27   The key is what a feature learns from the letters it reads. Averaging over every other letter contracts
28   its correlation with the ﬁnal count by a factor λ < 1 per unseen letter. Near initialization, the
29   output is dominated by terms that each read one letter; combining more letters costs powers of the
30   initialization scale (Figure 1b). Final answers thus supply very little credit to the features the network
31   can readily change. A displayed count and the current letter, however, expose the next update. The
32   two paths are
         outcome:     hidden letters → few-letter features decorrelate → tiny credit → plateau,
         step:        displayed count → local innovation → accessible credit → learned transition → solution.

101   The network reads few letters at a time. Scale every attention score by ζ ∈ [0, 1] and expand

134   4 Which solution training reaches
135   Small initial credit alone does not imply slow learning: the parameters might reach a region with a
136   stronger signal. We ask whether outcome ﬂow can get there quickly, and whether step ﬂow can turn
137   its local signal into a learned transition.

208   5 Experiments
209   The experiments ask where the explanation survives a change of optimizer, initialization or task, and
210   where it stops being enough. They use ﬁnite-step training; the AdamW runs have learned absolute
211   positions without the relative bias. Protocols are in Appendix E.

212   Does outcome escape exponentially slowly? The plateau proof concerns continuous time and a
213   small ball. We ﬁrst check whether its escape mechanism is visible with ﬁnite updates. In a compact
214   model built to approximate the theorems’ assumptions, with full-batch gradient descent in ﬂoat64
215   and no ReLU sign change on any input along the trajectory, the outcome exit time grows at least as
216   fast as λ−n , both from the ball Bε of Theorem 3 and at a displacement of 0.1εkθ̃k, while the step
217   exit time stays constant (Figure 3a, Table 7), because the step loss can leave the ball by ﬁtting the
218   count-only prior; halving the update length changes the exit time by less than 1%. At initialization,
219   99.6% of the Jacobian’s energy is at degree 0 (Figure 1b), and the answer-dependent gradient decays
220   by a ﬁtted 0.48–0.51 per letter at λ = 0.5 over three seeds (Table 9).
221   Under AdamW, with a zero readout and paired seeds, at n = 16, K = 2 and 8k updates, step
222   training reaches 93.1% exact accuracy (8 of 10 seeds above 90%) and outcome training 49.8%
223   (none). Across 120 counting runs at n = 6–16 with the default readout, outcome training solves
224   none and step training 60 within 8k updates. Step training at ε = 0.02 is slow rather than stuck, as
225   its ε6 rule credit suggests: every step run of Table 11(a) is solved by 16k updates, and at ε = 0.1,
226   where ε6 is 1.6 × 104 times larger, every step seed solves within 8k. From initialization through 6k
227   outcome updates, at most 2.4 × 10−6 of the Jacobian’s energy lies above degree 1 (Appendix E.3).
228   Outcome failure is not universal at moderate λn : for counting modulo 3 at n = 8 (λn ≈ 0.036),
229   outcome training solves two of three seeds within 32k updates.

230   Does outcome learn only the accessible low-degree prior? Near-chance accuracy can hide some
231   learning. To ﬁnd out what outcome training has learned, we compare its accuracy with the prediction
232   available from the start count alone. Varying the alphabet sets λ = 1 − A2 . Over 20 settings of
233   n ∈ {6, . . . , 16} and λ ∈ {0.5, . . . , 0.9}, outcome accuracy has Spearman correlation 0.91 with λn
                                                                                   n
234   and −0.36 with n alone. Most of this correlation is the state-only prior 1+λ2 , which predicting from
235   the start count achieves: cell means lie between 2 points below and 7 points above it (median +0.2),
236   and the excess has Spearman correlation only 0.15 with λn (Figure 3b,c). Once λn ≤ 0.04, every
237   cell mean is within 1.5 points of the prior. Once λn is small, these runs show little improvement
238   beyond that accessible prior. Together with the Jacobian measurements, this supports the picture of
239   training that stays sensitive mainly to degree-0 and 1 features.

260   6 Related work
261   Intermediate supervision. Scratchpads and process supervision [Nye et al., 2021, Uesato et al.,
262   2022, Wei et al., 2022, Lightman et al., 2023] have been analysed through task decomposition [Wies
263   et al., 2023], locality [Prystawski et al., 2023, Abbe et al., 2024] and expressivity [Feng et al., 2023,
264   Merrill and Sabharwal, 2024, Malach, 2024]; outcome supervision need not be statistically harder
265   [Jia et al., 2025], and chain supervision can improve sample complexity [Hanneke et al., 2026, Wen
266   et al., 2025]; Wen et al. [2025] trace the gain to sparse dependence between tokens, which the
267   running count makes local. We study how a ﬁxed network distributes gradient credit.

268   Gradient signal for hard targets. Shalev-Shwartz et al. [2017] show exponentially weak target-
269   dependent gradients over families of composed targets. We ask how one ﬁxed target supplies credit
270   to a concrete Transformer, by tracking which letters each network term reads. Staircase and leap
271   analyses [Abbe et al., 2021, 2022, 2023] characterize when SGD learns high-degree targets; for
272   counting at λ > 0 every degree is present, and its size, not its absence, limits credit. Abbe et al.
273   [2025] prove that noisy gradient descent on a two-layer network stays at chance on high-degree par-
274   ities under Gaussian initialization, for the correlation loss; Theorem 3 concerns a Transformer under
275   cross-entropy, with time λ−n from the contraction of the target. Panigrahi et al. [2025] show that
276   intermediate teacher checkpoints supply low-degree signal for sparse parity; we analyse optimiza-
277   tion rather than sample complexity. Li and Mossel [2025] use an Efron–Stein decomposition of the
278   target; our proofs need no decomposition: they bound the overlap of the target with each network
279   term directly, by conditioning on the letters the term reads, and we use ANOVA degree spectra only
280   as a measurement (Appendix E.3). Closest in spirit, Hahn and Roﬁn [2024] show that Transformers
281   are biased toward low-sensitivity functions, through a sharpness argument on the loss landscape; we


                                                           8
282   bound gradient credit through the letters each network term reads, and add a time bound. Spectral
283   analyses of simplicity bias [Yang and Salman, 2019] ﬁnd kernel eigenvalues on the Boolean cube
284   that decay with degree, consistent with the low-degree Jacobian we measure.

285   Chain of thought for parity and permutations. Kim and Suzuki [2025] prove that a one-layer
286   Transformer with position-only attention and a ﬁxed nonlinearity learns parity in one teacher-forced
287   step, and that gradient methods fail without intermediate supervision for a parity drawn from a class.
288   We ask why one format succeeds and the other fails for one ﬁxed target in a model holding both
289   solutions, with the computation learned. Wang et al. [2025] give a statistical-query lower bound and
290   a curriculum result for compositions of hidden permutations; our displayed states permit constant-
291   depth executors. Counting modulo K is a cyclic-group automaton, for which Liu et al. [2023]
292   construct shallow shortcut Transformers; our outcome executor is such a shortcut, and the question
293   is whether training ﬁnds it.

294   7 Discussion and conclusion
295   7.1   What the paper establishes

296   The exact executors give us a useful starting point: this Transformer can carry out either computation.
297   The learning gap appears because the two formats give its parameters different information. Final-
298   answer credit contracts across the letters a feature does not read; from a zero readout, the resulting
299   loss ﬂoor keeps outcome ﬂow near chance for exponential time. A displayed count leaves a local
300   update to learn. Its leading count–letter coefﬁcient is nonzero for p < 12 under full-preﬁx attention,
301   and the structured gate turns that signal into a transition learned in polynomial time.
302   Joint training of scores, relative bias and readout solves 16 of 17 step runs, and transfer experi-
303   ments generally favor step supervision. These results push beyond the proof, but accessible credit,
304   convergence with supplied routing, and discovery of that routing remain different claims.

305   7.2   What remains open

306   The ﬁrst open question is how the network learns where to look. A nonzero degree-6 coefﬁcient does
307   not show that simultaneous ﬂow from random initialization ampliﬁes it into routing. Table 1 gives
308   ﬁnite-step evidence at ε = 0.3, but the positive theorem supplies the mask and structured directions.
309   The other restrictions matter too. Theorems 1 and 2 concern small initialization with a ﬁxed ReLU
310   pattern; Theorem 3 adds full-batch gradient ﬂow. The AdamW and routing experiments leave that
311   regime. Theorem 4 covers every K ≥ 2 and transitions t ≥ 2, but trains the gate readout along I−R,
312   together with the count readout and query and key; the value map and the rest of the network stay
                                                                                        log 1
313   ﬁxed. Its bound grows like ε−4 log 1ε , so the proved separation needs n of order log 1ε (Remark D.1).
                                                                                            λ
314   A full circulant gate readout for K ≥ 3 adds background terms of no ﬁxed sign to the gate velocity
315   (Appendix D.5). Every reduced ﬂow we integrated still converges (Table 5); we do not yet know
316   how to prove that broader result.
317   There are empirical limits as well. Two of three outcome seeds solve counting modulo 3 at n = 8
318   within 32k updates. On BIG-Bench Hard, outcome training succeeds at ﬁve objects and one step run
319   fails, reversing the pattern at three and seven even though the low-degree variance share is smaller at
320   ﬁve. We leave that reversal unexplained. The controlled credit analysis gives us a way to ask what
321   signal training receives, but does not yet predict the trajectories of pretrained models.

322   Conclusion. Showing intermediate states changes which parts of a computation supply useful
323   gradient credit. In counting, we can follow that change from a local signal to a separation in training
324   time. The next question is how a network discovers the relevant computational structure when
325   the routing is not supplied. Understanding that discovery would bring the mechanism closer to
326   explaining when intermediate supervision helps reasoning beyond a controlled task.

499   C Proofs of Theorems 1 to 3
500   The outcome argument has two parts. We ﬁrst ask what a feature can learn about the answer from the
501   letters it reads, then bound which such features appear in the network near initialization. Combining
502   the estimates gives a local loss ﬂoor. The ﬂow’s energy identity turns that ﬂoor into an escape-time
503   bound. For step credit, the decisive distinction is between ﬁtting the displayed-state prior and using
504   the current letter. The proofs below keep those two signals separate.
505   C.1   Conventions
